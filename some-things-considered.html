Superglue Tricks

Superglue + Baking Soda

The last thing I did, which was from a break I did, was that the side windshields have a long plexiglass hole where an Allen screw attaches it, and I was trying to see if I could mount my phone or other things there out of the way. The answer is yes, and no, because it doesn't work, and if I get adjustability then I hit the handlebars' turning radius. So I broke the second attachment point and plastic tab.

Having become somewhat of a wizard with superglue, I used that and baking soda. That makes a harder, more resilient plastic. I used this as a temporary fix while I ordered the stock replacement from AliExpress. However it worked so good I haven't replaced it.

I asked AI to explain the chemistry. Here's what's happening:

  • Catalyzed polymerization: Superglue cures when exposed to base molecules or moisture (hydroxyl ions). Baking soda acts as an aggressive alkaline catalyst. On contact, it forces the liquid cyanoacrylate to polymerize (link into long chemical chains) almost instantly instead of taking 30–60 seconds.
  • Composite structural matrix: The solid baking soda particles get encased directly within the fast-curing polymer matrix. This transforms the superglue from a thin layer between surfaces into a thick, rock-hard 3D composite material similar to acrylic plastic.

Superglue + Water

Another trick is a few drops of water will make superglue cure almost instantly. However this reaction is exothermic and will heat up a bit.

Again, AI on the chemistry:

  • Hydroxyl ion catalyst: Superglue requires a base (specifically hydroxyl ions, OH−) to kick off its curing process. While superglue normally relies on trace humidity in the air or moisture on surface boundaries to cure over tens of seconds, adding direct water introduces an abundance of moisture at once.
  • Instant polymerization: The liquid cyanoacrylate monomers rapidly chain together in a runaway reaction, solidifying the glue in under two seconds.
  • Exothermic heat release: Chemical bond formation releases energy. Because a reaction that usually takes 30 to 60 seconds happens in a fraction of a second, all that thermal energy is released at once. A single drop can get surprisingly hot to the touch, and larger pools can bubble, steam, or scorch sensitive surfaces.

Freezer Storage

Storing superglue is difficult because it'll harden. However, did you know that putting a used superglue sachet into a ziploc and storing it in the freezer will cause it not to harden? I found this out because I use superglue to hold together cuts I might need stitches for, or as butterflies, and I rarely use it all.

Motorcycle Wind Chill

So with the temperature rapidly changing and light fading I started to think about: is there a better way to know how to layer in my gear. First caveat is that I have all mesh summer gear, because it's hot where I'm at. I may get some not-so-mesh gear, but as I said before, having money has never been my problem, and I have great layers.

So what factors make it colder on a bike? Let's start with a 90 degree F day. Now if I'm doing 5-10mph in town I'm going to be hot, but 40mph and above quite comfortable. Does the temperature change? No it does not.

So what's happening is the same thing as on a windy day where I got hypothermic and hallucinated while trying to ascend a 14er in the White Mountains in Eastern California. The wind robs your body of the heat molecules around it, causing your body to try to work harder to heat the air, obviously it can't do this, and we get cold. This event is well known as the wind chill factor. Below I have used some dubious math and charts to consider speed vs temperature vs windshield vs not.

Now of course the size of the windshield will matter, as well as full fairings. Basically the more airflow around you, the more the perceived temperature change is going to happen at speed, not counting actual wind produced by the weather and not your throttle hand.

The Formula

The U.S. National Weather Service / Environment Canada wind chill equation, revised in 2001, gives the perceived temperature on bare skin:

WCT = 35.74 + 0.6215·T − 35.75·V0.16 + 0.4275·T·V0.16

Where T is ambient temperature (°F) and V is wind speed relative to your body (mph). On an open bike, that's your road speed.

Validity window: the formula is only defined for T ≤ 50°F and V ≥ 3 mph. Above 50°F there's still convective cooling at speed (which actually feels nice when it's hot), but the formula over-cools if you try to use it there, so summer rows just show ambient.

Shielded Variant (windshield / fairing)

A full windshield or fairing creates a low-velocity recirculation pocket in front of your torso. The air hitting your chest moves much slower than the bike moves through the air. Modeled as a simple velocity scaling:

Veffective = k · Vroad

Where k is the shield mitigation factor (dimensionless, 0 to 1):

  • 0.05 – 0.10 — tall touring windshield or full fairing (BMW RT, Gold Wing)
  • 0.10 – 0.20 — mid-size shield that reaches between chest and chin
  • 0.30 – 0.50 — small sport bubble, flyscreen
  • 1.00 — no shield (reduces to the base formula)

Covers torso only. Hands, head, and feet still see the full unshielded number. Grip warmers, a good helmet, and insulated boots matter more than the fairing for those zones.

Reading Notes

  • Numbers are "feels like" temperature on exposed skin, rounded to the nearest 1°F.
  • Summer rows (above 50°F) show ambient only — the NWS formula isn't valid there.
  • 0 mph columns are stationary rider in still air. A 20 mph gust while waiting at a light would still produce real wind chill, but that's not modeled here.
  • Delta underneath each cell shows how far below ambient the perceived temperature has dropped.
  • Formula source: NWS / Environment Canada joint revision, 2001. The shielded extension is an engineering simplification, not an official standard. A real fairing produces turbulent, non-uniform flow that this scalar model only approximates.
