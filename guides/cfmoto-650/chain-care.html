Cleaning and Lubing the Chain

Kerosene, a grunge brush, and 20 minutes of your life every 500 miles.

Chain maintenance is universal — this applies to any chain-drive bike, not just the CFMoto. Skip it and you'll be replacing a chain and sprockets at 15,000 miles instead of 30,000.

Know Your Chain

Two types of chains exist here: sealed and non-sealed. Sealed chains come in two flavors — O-ring and X-ring — named after the cross-section of the rubber ring sandwiched between the inner and outer side plates. Both do the same job: trap factory grease on the pin and bushing forever. X-ring is just an evolution of the O-ring with less friction (two smaller sealing contact points instead of one squished one). Non-sealed (standard) chains have no rubber rings — the internal bearing is open to the world.

Basically, if you see any rubber ring between the side plates, you have a sealed chain. All chains need cleaning. Sealed chains don't need lube reaching the internal bearings — those are sealed and greased for life at the factory — but they absolutely do need lube on the rollers (which spin against the sprocket teeth) and to keep the rubber rings supple so they don't dry out and crack.

Keep in mind: what matters most is the rollers. The outer side links aren't doing any sliding work, but the rollers are what engage the sprockets, every rotation.

What to Clean With

You can use many things, but what you should use is kerosene. It dissolves road oil, old lube, and dirt like nothing else, it's dirt cheap, and it's what the major chain manufacturers (DID in particular) specifically recommend.

Do NOT use these on a sealed chain:

Gasoline, carb cleaner, brake cleaner, strong degreasers, or wire brushes. All of them can swell or tear the O/X-rings. Once the rings fail, the internal grease escapes and the chain is cooked.

Get a spray bottle — new, or an old one cleaned out. Look for HDPE (High-Density Polyethylene, recycling code #2); #4 (LDPE) works too but isn't as rigid. I cleaned out an old cleaning bottle for mine and labeled it clearly, as any old lab rat would. In fact, always label containers clearly if you're going to store something in them — it's just better for everyone, mostly you.

What You'll Need

  • Cardboard (to protect the wheel and swingarm from overspray)
  • Two old rags — one for cleaning, one for the lube pass
  • A grunge brush — 3-sided is best (the Tirox Grunge Brush is the standard)
  • Kerosene in a labeled HDPE spray bottle
  • Chain lube — I'm using Maxima Chain Wax right now. Cheap, and highly recommended.
  • Nitrile gloves and decent ventilation (kerosene is a skin irritant, and the fumes aren't fun indoors)

The Procedure

1. Get the bike up

Put the bike on a center stand or paddock stand — you need to be able to rotate the rear wheel freely.

2. Protect the wheel

Slide cardboard between the chain and the wheel to catch overspray. Kerosene on a brake rotor is a very bad time.

3. Mark your starting point

Either make a note of where on the chain you're starting, or find the paint mark many chains come with from the factory. Without a marker, you'll either miss a section or do a lap and a half for nothing.

4. Spray, brush, wipe

Start spraying kerosene. After a round or two (depending on how dirty it is), work the grunge brush in. You should see gunk loosen up. Alternate spraying, brushing, and wiping with the rag until the chain is sparkling clean — no black gunk left. Ideally you want to see fresh metal.

5. Wait

Once it's clean, wait at least 20–30 minutes for the kerosene to fully evaporate before lubing. Longer if it's cool or humid out. Lube applied to wet kerosene just gets diluted and flung off.

Lubing

Same cardboard trick to protect the wheel. Apply your lube to the inner side of the chain — the side facing the sprockets — because that's where centrifugal force flings it onto the rollers and works it in. The outer side doesn't do any sliding work.

If you're on a non-sealed chain, use more lube and make sure it penetrates everywhere.

Give the wheel a few spins, wait 5–10 minutes for the solvent carrier in the lube to flash off, then wipe the excess clean. Excess lube only attracts grit.

One More Thing

If the chain's really filthy — mud, salt, that kind of thing — you may want to wash the bike (chain area included) with soap and water first, then follow up with kerosene. Just don't wash after lubing. Soap emulsifies oil, kerosene dissolves it, and all your work goes down the driveway. Keep soap, water, and kerosene away from a freshly lubed chain.

How Often?

  • Street riding: every 300–600 miles
  • After every rain ride
  • After any off-road or gravel use

Happy wrenching.
