CFMoto 650 Adventura — 3,000-Mile Oil Screen Service

Dropping the oil pan to clean the oil screen — including the exhaust and header dance to get there. Field notes from a 2022 model.

Overview

Cleaning the oil screen means dropping the oil pan. To get the pan off, I had to remove the slip-on muffler, which meant pulling the rear brake-side foot peg assembly, both lower side plastics, and the brackets that bolt those plastics onto the oil pan.

This writeup is from the brake (right) side of the bike. Hardware sizes called out below (8 mm, 10 mm, 12 mm, 13 mm) are the wrench/socket sizes I actually used.

1. "Missing" bolt in the crankcase — it's a machine hole

In case you're like me and spot what looks like a missing bolt in the crankcase, don't worry about it — it's a machine hole and it's supposed to be empty. The arrow in the second photo points right at it.

2. Brake-side lower plastics and foot peg assembly

Starting on the brake side of the bike: remove the lower plastic (three Allen bolts) and the mid-side plastic (one bolt plus two tabs). That gives you access to the foot peg assembly, which is held on by three Allen bolts — one is hidden behind a black panel — and a 13 mm nut at the rear where it attaches to the tubular frame.

3. Supporting the foot peg, exhaust bolts, and saving hardware

With the foot peg loose, I used a velcro tie and an Allen key to hold it up and out of the way so I didn't have to disconnect the brake line.

The two bolts holding the exhaust: the longer one is 12 mm and goes all the way through to the chain side — you'll need to hold a 13 mm nut on the far side to break it loose. The other mount is 13 mm. At this point I set the bike on a rear paddock stand to make everything easier to reach.

The last couple of photos in this section are of washers and small bits — I take pictures of this stuff because you never know what's going to fall off while you're working, and it's nice to have a reference for reassembly.

4. Loosen the clamp and pull the exhaust

Loosen the clamping ring and the exhaust slides out with a few taps. These photos show the exhaust free of the bike and what the access area looks like with it out of the way.

5. Remove side brackets and the oil filter

Next: pull the brackets on each side that attach the plastic lower bash-guard pieces (10 mm bolts), then take the oil filter out so you can get at the pan. This is where I started dropping the fasteners into labeled bags attached to each bracket so I knew which bolts came from which side.

6. Oil pan bolts (8 mm)

With the brackets and filter out of the way, you can now see the ten 8 mm oil pan bolts. They're all the same length. A few sit in deep recesses — for those, you'll want a stubby wrench or a deep socket. I almost had to make a parts-store run, but luckily I'm my own bike mechanic too, and I had a tri-wrench that got me there.

7. The supervisor

The supervisor, ever diligent, making sure I was doing things correctly.

8. Freeing the pan

With the oil pan screws out, I used a small claw hammer on the chain side to gently pry the pan away so it fell free. The rubber flange has a V-groove that looked like it was meant to seat around a round tube visible in the next photos.

9. Pan dropped — orientation notes and a mystery piece

With the pan off I took a round of pictures and tried to note the orientation of everything hanging down and sticking out — anything that might not be obvious an hour later when I'm putting it back together. There was one mystery piece: a small black plastic end that I couldn't immediately place. My first guess was that it belonged on the end of the metal tube dangling down (it looked like it had separated from the filter flange, the black housing above). More on that in a few sections — turns out that first guess was right, but getting it to seat flush was its own adventure.

10. Cleaning the screen and the pan

I drained the excess oil out of the pan and took the screen off. The two bolts holding it on were pretty tight. Cleaned the screen in kerosene. For the pan's mating surface I used a Brillo pad to work off the old bits of gasket that were still stuck down.

In the next photo you can see where I tried to place that mystery piece — turned out this wasn't quite the right spot, but it got me closer to figuring out where it actually belonged.

11. Where the hell does this go

I eventually figured out that yes, the mystery piece goes on the end of that dangling tube — but to seat it flush from below I needed to drop the header to get a straight shot at it.

12. Dropping the header (the key to the whole reassembly)

I didn't get a picture of the plastic radiator guard coming off, but it's three screws and a reverse pop-out plastic rivet. I also had to undo a bit of my crash bar cage to get the header out. The header bolts took a 12 mm. Once the header was down, everything on the pan side went back together cleanly.

13. Reassembling the pan

Once I had everything lined up (pan, gasket, dangling tube with its mystery cap finally home), I started one bolt through the gasket a couple of threads, then the opposite corner the same way, and kept going corner-to-corner until I could see the pan was lined up square against the case. Then I tightened each one a little at a time, working around, until everything was snug and the gasket was well seated.

On torque: I didn't use a torque wrench for this job. The pan bolts and the other critical fasteners — header bolts especially — went in by feel: snug plus a little bit. If I ever hunt down proper torque specs for the pan and the header, I'll add them here.

14. Wrap-up and first start

Long day. The supervisor said it was dinner time. For the rest I put the drain plug and oil filter back on, reinstalled the brackets that hold the lower plastic bash guards and the headers, refilled with oil, and checked for leaks. Moment of truth: it fired, no weird noises, idled for five minutes clean. Finished buttoning up the plastics and I was done.
